\begin{abstract}
We predict self-reported coronary heart disease from incomplete health-survey
answers. Our NumPy-only system combines histogram-based boosted trees with
regularized logistic regression trained by Newton's method. A common three-fold
evaluation compares interactions, nonlinear trees and probability
blending. The delivered blend obtains development F1 of
$\FinalFOne\pm\FinalStd$. We report the exact preprocessing and decision rule,
distinguish development estimates from independent evaluation, and provide
saved weights and a reproducible prediction script.
\end{abstract}
\section{Task and Data}
The provided BRFSS dataset contains 328\,135 labeled respondents, 109\,379 test
respondents and 321 input variables. The positive class represents a reported
heart attack or coronary heart disease diagnosis, rather than a future clinical
event. Only 28\,975 training examples (8.83\%) are positive: always predicting
the negative class gives 91.17\% accuracy but F1 of zero. We therefore evaluate
positive-class F1, $2TP/(2TP+FP+FN)$.

Missing entries account for 44.8\% of the training matrix, and 147 variables
are missing in more than half the rows. Survey codes also need interpretation:
for example, \texttt{PHYSHLTH}=88 means no unhealthy days, whereas 77 and 99
indicate nonresponses~\cite{cdc}. We use the supplied data only.
\section{Preprocessing and Models}
\subsection{Logistic features}
The logistic encoder excludes constant variables, columns with over 80\%
missing entries and eight bookkeeping fields: dates, months, year,
\texttt{SEQNO}, \texttt{\_PSU} and \texttt{\_STSTR}.
Column-specific rules map known nonresponses to missing,
``none'' codes to zero and coded frequencies, height and weight to numerical
units. Other columns use the recorded nonresponse-code heuristic.

Variables with at most \textbf{30 distinct observed values} are one-hot encoded;
\texttt{\_STATE} is always categorical. Levels observed fewer than 100 times
share an ``other'' category with missing values when present in training;
unseen values use this category if available, otherwise the reference level.
The most frequent level is the reference. Continuous variables are median-imputed, clipped to
training percentiles 1 and 99, and standardized. We add distinct missingness
flags, including flags for dropped columns with up to 99\% missingness,
and indicators of original ``none'' codes. Finally, we standardize squares of
the ten continuous columns most correlated with the target and pairwise
products of the 25 most correlated encoded columns. Every statistic and
feature selection is learned within the training fold.
\subsection{Regularized logistic regression}
For $y_i\in\{0,1\}$ and encoded $x_i$, we minimize
\begin{equation}
L(w)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_i[\log(1+e^{x_i^\top w})-y_ix_i^\top w]
       +10^{-4}\|w\|_2^2 .
\end{equation}
The delivered implementation penalizes \emph{all} coefficients, including the
intercept. Newton updates solve the Hessian system, with backtracking and a
maximum of 15 iterations. The Hessian is accumulated in blocks of 20\,000
rows. Loss calculations are numerically stable. The six required course
methods remain available separately in \texttt{implementations.py}.
\subsection{Boosted trees and final blend}
Our implementation follows gradient boosting~\cite{friedman}, using first and
second derivatives of logistic loss. Each feature is discretized into at most
63 bins plus a missing bin; splits learn the direction of missing values.
Trees have depth four, learning rate 0.1, leaf regularization 1 and minimum
child Hessian sum 5. Each tree samples half the training rows and half the
features (seed 42). Leaf updates use $-G/(H+1)$.

The final decision combines the two probabilities:
\begin{align}
p&=0.7p_{\mathrm{trees}}+0.3p_{\mathrm{logistic}},\notag\\
\hat y&=\begin{cases}+1&p\ge0.225,\\-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\end{align}
We refit on all labeled rows with 209 trees and the logistic encoding above.
The experiment below reproduces the fold-wise development procedure used
to select this configuration.
\section{Evaluation and Results}
\subsection{Common protocol}
All rows in Table~\ref{tab:results} use the \emph{same three shuffled folds}
(NumPy \texttt{RandomState}, seed 42; not stratified). Encoders and model weights
are refitted inside each training fold. For tree early stopping, 10\% of that
fold is held out; training stops after 50 iterations without a validation-loss
improvement. The original retained tree counts were 183, 171 and 216; their
mean multiplied by 1.1 gives 209 trees for the full-data refit.

We pool out-of-fold (OOF) probabilities and search thresholds from 0.02 to
0.98 in steps of 0.005. The delivered blend uses its fixed threshold 0.225.
Reported uncertainty is the population standard deviation of the three fold
F1 scores, not a confidence interval. Model settings, the blend weight and
threshold were selected on these development data, so these are \emph{not}
unbiased estimates on a final untouched holdout.

The raw-feature baseline uses ridge regression ($\lambda=10^{-4}$), with
training-only mean imputation and standardization. Encoded Newton is the
linear probabilistic baseline. Adding interactions changes F1 by
$\InteractionsGain$; boosted trees improve over that logistic variant by
$\TreeGain$. Blending changes F1 by $\BlendGain$ relative to trees alone.
Small changes should be interpreted against fold variability, not as proof
of superiority. The tree/logistic comparison also changes the representation;
only the interaction and blend ablations isolate a single added component.
These results do not establish a universally optimal category cutoff.

\section{Discussion and Reproducibility}
Our main finding is that nonlinear trees improve on an encoded logistic model,
while blending gives a smaller additional change. Survey coding remains an
assumption: heuristic nonresponse detection may misinterpret valid categories.
Repeated development-set selection can overestimate generalization. The data
are self-reported and cross-sectional; the model is neither a causal analysis
nor a validated prospective screening tool.

We recorded an earlier AIcrowd F1 of 0.455 in our experiment log.
The original uploaded CSV was unavailable for comparison, so we do not claim
that this score has been independently verified for the regenerated file.
The repository includes the delivered weights, CSV fingerprints and the full
training code. \texttt{python run.py} generates predictions from the saved
weights; \texttt{python run.py --train} refits the recorded model. The validation
script exports per-fold results and OOF predictions, from which the final
figures and table are generated automatically. This keeps the report,
configuration and executable submission consistent.
