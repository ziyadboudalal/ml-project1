\section{Introduction}

The training set has 328\,135 respondents and the test set 109\,379, each
described by 321 answers from a telephone health survey. The label is 1 if
the respondent was diagnosed with MICHD (heart attack or angina), and 8.8\% of
the training labels are positive. Predicting the majority class gives 91\%
accuracy but an F1 of 0, so all models are compared by the F1 of the positive
class, which is also the leaderboard metric.

\section{Data and Feature Engineering}
\label{sec:features}

Most columns are not measurements but answer codes. For example, general
health (\texttt{GENHLTH}) takes values 1 (excellent) to 5 (poor), plus
7 = ``don't know'' and 9 = ``refused''. A single weight on the raw value
treats ``refused'' as worse than ``poor''. Many questions are also asked only
to a subset of respondents: 147 of the 321 columns are missing in more than
half of the rows.

We build the following features. All statistics (levels, quantiles, means)
are computed on the training rows only, inside each cross-validation fold.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Dropped:} identifiers, interview dates, state, survey
    weights, constant columns and columns with less than 1\% observed values.
  \item \textbf{Categorical columns} ($\le 60$ distinct values): one-hot
    encoding, with answer codes kept as their own levels. Levels seen in less
    than 0.2\% of rows are merged into the reference level, which is dropped
    to avoid collinearity with the bias.
  \item \textbf{Continuous columns:} frequent answer codes (e.g.\ 7777,
    9999) become indicators. The remaining values are clipped to their 1st and
    99th percentiles, standardized, and expanded with a squared term
    (polynomial basis of degree 2).
  \item \textbf{Missing indicators} for every column with missing values.
  \item \textbf{Interactions:} pairwise products of the 20 encoded columns
    most correlated with the label, skipping pairs from the same question and
    products that are almost always zero.
\end{itemize}
This gives about 1\,230 columns without interactions and 1\,410 with them.

\section{Models}

\subsection{Baselines}
The six required methods are implemented in \texttt{implementations.py}. As
baselines, we use ridge regression (labels in $\{-1,1\}$, $\lambda=10^{-4}$)
and regularized logistic regression with gradient descent ($\lambda=10^{-4}$,
$\gamma=0.15$, 300 iterations) on the raw columns, mean-imputed and
standardized.

\subsection{Regularized logistic regression with Newton's method}

We minimize the mean logistic loss with an $L_2$ penalty on all weights
except the bias $w_0$:
\begin{equation}
  L(w) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\log\!\left(1+e^{x_i^\top w}\right)
  - y_i\, x_i^\top w\right] + \lambda \sum_{j\ge 1} w_j^2 .
\end{equation}
Gradient descent needs a tuned step size and many iterations with 1\,400
columns of very different scales. Newton's method uses the Hessian
$H = \frac{1}{n} X^\top S X + 2\lambda I$, with
$S = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma(x_i^\top w)(1-\sigma(x_i^\top w)))$, and updates
$w \leftarrow w - H^{-1}\nabla L(w)$. It converges in about 10 iterations.
$X^\top S X$ is accumulated over blocks of rows so that memory stays bounded,
and a step that increases the loss is halved. We use $\lambda=3\cdot10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Decision threshold}
With 8.8\% positives, few predicted probabilities exceed 0.5. We therefore
choose the threshold $t$ that maximizes F1 on the out-of-fold probabilities,
by an exact search over all distinct scores (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}). The
selected threshold is $t = 0.20$.

\subsection{Validation protocol}
All models are evaluated with the same stratified 5-fold cross-validation
(seed 2026). For each fold, the feature encoder, the selection of
interaction columns and the weights are fitted on the four training folds
only. One threshold is chosen on the pooled out-of-fold probabilities, and we
report the mean and standard deviation of the F1 of the five folds at that
threshold. Our model is refitted on all labeled data with the same
threshold.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} compares the baselines with each step of our model.
Encoding the features adds $+0.022$, by far the largest gain. Interactions add
$+0.002$, which is within one standard deviation across folds. The
cross-validated F1 of the submitted model (0.434) is close to its test F1 on
AIcrowd (0.442, accuracy 0.873), so the validation did not overfit.

\subsection{What did not work}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Default threshold.} At $t=0.5$ our model predicts too
    few positives: F1 drops from 0.434 to 0.231 (Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}).
  \item \textbf{Changing the loss on raw features.} Ridge regression and
    logistic regression on the same raw columns give the same F1 (0.410 vs
    0.411): the bottleneck is the representation, not the model.
  \item \textbf{More interactions.} 12 interaction columns give 0.433 and 20
    give 0.434. With 30 columns (about 430 products) the design matrix reaches
    2.2\,GB and did not fit in memory on our 8\,GB machine. Plain Newton steps
    also diverged with a singular Hessian, because some products are almost
    always zero (incompatible answers). Removing these products and halving
    steps that increase the loss fixed the divergence, but we did not go
    beyond 20 columns.
  \item \textbf{Tuning $\lambda$.} Values from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-3}$ all give
    F1 between 0.433 and 0.434, within one standard deviation: with 328k rows
    and 1\,410 columns, regularization barely matters.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

The largest weights of our model (Fig.~\ref{fig:weights}) match
known risk factors. Female sex, excellent general health and the absence of
stroke or COPD lower the predicted risk. Poor general health, kidney disease
and having been advised by a doctor raise it. Since the weights of one-hot
levels are relative to the reference level of the same question, they should
be read as contrasts, not as absolute effects.
